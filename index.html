From Europe / open source, accelerated

The same browser, at a different pace.

Galileo is a small European team that takes important open-source software that has slowed down and moves it again. AI writes the volume; humans hold every gate. Our first project is a web browser, forked from Servo — in under a month it went from barely opening pages to an almost-working browser.

Where we start

First, the praise. It is earned.

Mozilla Research started Servo in 2012. Over the years, open-source maintainers — and later the Linux Foundation — turned it into a serious browser engine in Rust: a real rendering pipeline, serious standards work, an architecture that is a pleasure to build on. People have been building browsers on Servo for a long time, and the work is real.

The pace is the problem. Servo does not accept AI-generated contributions — a fair rule, which protects maintainers from code nobody can explain, test, or defend. It also means a volunteer project answers a funded industry with its hands and its spare time. Galileo is the counter-experiment: AI generates the volume, and the volume has to earn its place — with tests, retained evidence, and human review. If it works, the same method applies to whatever deserves to move next.

The pace, on one clock

Twenty-three days, measured.

The fork landed on 26 July 2026 on top of a 15.2-million-line Servo snapshot. The import counts as zero; everything after counts as work. Upstream Servo kept moving at its natural pace on the same clock.

2,262 commits966,582 lines changed×27 on engine code×4.3 total churn vs upstream

Lines changed is not quality, and we would never pass it off as one. But on the same clock, it is the clearest picture we have of what a small, focused, AI-assisted team does to a backlog that a bigger project could not get to.

Where it stands

From “it opens a page” to “it is a browser.”

In under a month the fork grew from a test shell into something with a desktop face and a product skeleton. Part of it is usable today. All of it is in the repository, and the parts that do not work are named as such.

A desktop browser, not a shell

Tabs, navigation, profiles, private mode, history, bookmarks, downloads, permissions, settings, and session restore are real surfaces now, not mockups.

Real media work

GStreamer playback paths and Media Source Extensions fixes are moving YouTube-style streaming forward — one of the hardest inherited gaps in the engine.

Turnstile, tested

The official Cloudflare Turnstile canary passes in all three modes, headless and headed. Production challenges are Cloudflare's risk decision — we will not fake fingerprints to pass them.

A measured web baseline

286 / 286 subtests in the Phase 0 Web Platform shard, where the same upstream reference passes 246. Narrow, real, and growing — not a compatibility score.

An extension runtime

Manifest V2 and V3 entrypoints run through a permission-reviewed, browser-owned host. Ad blocking is designed into the browser, not bolted on after.

A public inventory

68 capability rows, each with its evidence and its limits. The gaps live on the roadmap where you can read them, not in a footnote.

The method

Humans own the gate. AI does the volume.

We are not asking you to trust a chatbot. We are showing that a small team can direct more implementation, testing, and iteration than it could do alone — and that the direction is still human.

01 / accelerate

Backlog becomes working code.

Small, bounded tasks are easy to parallelise, easy to review, easy to replace. The repository stays public the whole time, so speed can be checked, not believed.

02 / verify

Claims have to earn their page.

Focused tests, retained artifacts, pinned commits. A line of code means nothing until a test says what it does — and the test stays where anyone can re-run it.

03 / publish

The gaps stay visible.

Security, speed, packaging, compatibility: where the evidence stops, the page says so. We would rather show an empty bar than hide it in adjectives.

After the browser

The browser is the first of many.

A faster browser is not the goal. The goal is the method, proven on the hardest project in open source. Then the same team points it at the next: an advanced image editor that does not phone home, an email client that treats your inbox as yours, the small tools the big companies stopped shipping. We are not naming them yet. The blur is the point.

Why now

You are renting your browser.

One store removed the world's biggest ad-blocker because a manifest version made it inconvenient. The same store decides which extensions exist, which trackers a page gets, and where your data goes. A browser you cannot own is a service you are renting.

We are a European team, and we build in Europe. This is not a European product — it is for everyone who wants a browser whose rules they can read. And when the method is proven, the same logic applies to the rest of the software that keeps the open ecosystem alive: the parts that matter, move too slowly, and quietly push people back to closed tools.

The relationship

We did not hijack a browser. We accelerated one.

Servo is MPL-2.0 open source, built to be embedded, extended, and forked. We took the code, kept the license, and we will keep sending generally useful fixes back — tested, and marked as AI-assisted where they are. The maintainers accept what they want, and that is their right; the work stays public either way.

At some point the fork will be Galileo through and through — engine name, product, security model. When that happens, the rename finishes and we go our separate way. Until then, the fork stays useful to the project it came from.
